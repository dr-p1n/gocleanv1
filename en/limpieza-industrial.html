Industrial cleaning for plants and warehouses

Cleaning that runs at the plant's pace, not the other way around.

Cleaning a plant is not cleaning an office on a larger scale. What comes off is different, grease and oil and process dust, the surfaces tolerate different things, and the setting carries risks that do not exist in an office building: moving machinery, hot surfaces and stored energy.

This page is about the work itself: what gets cleaned, with what, and in which window. If what you are looking for is how cleaning is organized across a terminal or a logistics park, that is covered under ports and logistics zones.

What gets cleaned in an industrial plant?

There are four fronts, each with its own equipment and its own frequency. All four are rarely contracted at once.

What sets the scope is not the size of the plant but what it makes: a food plant and a metalworking shop get dirty in ways that have nothing in common.

Floors and production areas

This is the steadiest front. Grease, oil and process residue build up, and the floor has to end up clean without ending up slippery, which is the hard part. Treating the material itself, when the concrete is polished or sealed, is handled separately under floor care.

Machinery and equipment

The outside of the machines, their bases and the area around them, where whatever production throws off collects. What is touched and what is not is a line set out in writing before the work starts, not as it goes along.

Overhead structure, ducts and hoods

Beams, light fixtures, extraction ducts and hoods collect process dust for months with nobody looking at them, until it falls onto the line or shows up in an audit. They are worked on with the plant stopped, from scaffolding or a lift.

Channels, grease traps and drains

This is the front most often left out of the contract and the first one to cause trouble: a clogged drain sends the wash water back onto the production floor.

How is industrial degreasing different from washing a floor?

The product is chosen for the surface and for what is sitting on it, not for the type of premises.

An alkaline degreaser that works on concrete can stain aluminum or attack an epoxy coating. Animal fat in a food plant does not come off the way mineral oil does in a machine shop. And water temperature and pressure count as much as the chemical: at too much pressure, water spreads the grease instead of carrying it away and drives it where it should not go.

That is why degreasing starts by identifying the surface and the type of residue, and by testing on a small area before it is applied across the floor.

What gets done during a plant shutdown?

A scheduled shutdown is the only window for reaching what cannot be touched while the line is running.

These are short jobs with a closing time: overhead structure, the inside of equipment, drains, and the areas behind the machinery. They are planned backward, from the hour the plant has to start up again, and it is worth agreeing in advance what gets left for next time if the clock runs out.

A major shutdown, a relocation or a cleanup after an incident is no longer a recurring program but a project with its own schedule and crew: that is covered under special projects.

Who cleans the machinery, the operator or the cleaning staff?

This is worth settling before signing, because the answer decides who answers for it if something is damaged.

The sensible rule is that cleaning staff work on external surfaces and on the area around the equipment, and that anything calling for a machine to be opened, taken apart or de-energized is done or authorized by the plant's own maintenance staff, with the equipment locked out. That boundary goes into the scope, machine by machine if need be.

There is also a step beforehand that does not get skipped: the plant's safety induction and the work permit where one applies. The facility sets the rules, not the provider.

Where do the wash water and the waste end up?

The water coming off a degreasing job carries grease and chemical, so it is not ordinary cleaning water.

It normally goes through the grease trap or through the treatment the plant already has, with solid residue separated at the point where it is generated. What cannot happen is for it to end up in a storm drain.

Hazardous waste, process chemicals and controlled substances have their own chain, in the hands of the generator or an authorized waste manager, and the cleaning service does not replace it. Ordinary office and break-room waste is covered under additional facility services.
