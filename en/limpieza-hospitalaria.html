Hospital cleaning and clinical biosafety

The hospital cleaning protocol, area by area: what is done, how, and what record it leaves.

Hospital cleaning has rules that commercial cleaning does not cover: the protocol changes with the risk level of each area, work runs from the least contaminated surface to the most contaminated, and every intervention leaves a record. This page explains how that work is organized.

Go Services operates as an extension of the clinical team, with staff trained in hospital biosafety and documentation ready for audit. If you run a health facility and need to decide how to contract this service, that guide is at hospital and healthcare cleaning.

What does hospital cleaning include in each type of area?

The risk level of each area sets the product, the frequency and the equipment. These are the four situations that change the protocol most.

Critical areas

Operating rooms, intensive care units and emergency rooms, where the patient is most vulnerable to infection. The working rule is to clean from the least to the most contaminated surfaces, floor last, to use cloths and tools dedicated to the area, and to disinfect high-touch points with particular care: bed rails, fixed equipment, switches and handles.

Semi-critical areas

Inpatient rooms, consulting rooms, laboratories and imaging areas. They combine daily concurrent cleaning with disinfection of the surfaces touched most: bedside tables, call buttons, stretchers and handles. Risk is moderate here, and it is the frequency of the rounds that keeps it that way.

Non-critical areas

Corridors, waiting rooms, administrative offices and service areas. Routine maintenance cleaning applies, with attention to touch points in waiting rooms, where patients and companions pass through all day.

Isolation rooms

Rooms for patients with diseases transmitted by contact, droplets or air. The rule is to clean them at the end of the round so contamination is not carried into other areas, with the protective equipment the type of isolation calls for and with tools that never leave the room.

What is terminal disinfection and when is it done?

It is the full cleaning and disinfection of a room or area once the patient leaves, whether by discharge or transfer, and before the next one arrives. It covers furniture, high-touch surfaces, fixed equipment, bathroom and floor, and is done once bedding and waste have been removed.

It differs from concurrent disinfection, which is done daily with the patient in the room. After an isolation patient, terminal disinfection is more demanding, because the room cannot be reused until it has been released.

What is the difference between cleaning, disinfecting and sterilizing?

The three terms are often used interchangeably, and they are different processes:

  • Cleaning. Removes dirt and organic matter from the surface. It comes first: a disinfectant does not work well on a dirty surface.
  • Disinfecting. Reduces microorganisms on the surface to a safe level, using a product applied for the contact time stated on its technical data sheet.
  • Sterilizing. Destroys all microbial life, spores included. It is done on instruments in the hospital's sterilization department, not on floors or surfaces.

Surface cleaning goes as far as disinfection. If a proposal offers to “sterilize” an area, ask what exactly it means.

How is bio-infectious waste handled?

It is separated at the point where it is generated: bio-infectious waste goes into red bags and sharps into rigid containers. It is never mixed with general waste, and it is removed along the internal route each facility defines, with staff wearing the protective equipment required.

Final disposal falls to an authorized operator, under the health regulations in force.

What documentation is left after each intervention?

Each intervention is logged with the time, the zone, the product applied and the person responsible. Those records are organized into checklists by area and a report for each shift, so that an audit, internal or by the Ministry of Health, can reconstruct what was done, where and by whom.

That traceability is what separates hospital cleaning from commercial cleaning. It is also what lets you compare proposals: if a provider cannot show how it records each intervention, the protocol exists only on paper. For disinfection outside clinical settings, see disinfection and sanitization.
