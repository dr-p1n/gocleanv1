Floor polishing and care

Every material calls for a different treatment. Here is the guide, floor by floor.

A dull, scratched or stained floor signals neglect before anyone looks at the walls. In hotels, showrooms and corporate buildings, the state of the floor tracks directly with how the whole place is judged.

The most common mistake when buying this service is asking for "polishing" across the board. Polishing, crystallization, sealing and waxing are different processes, and applying the wrong one can dull the surface, leave it slippery or shorten the life of the material. The first step is identifying what you actually have underfoot.

What treatment does each type of floor need?

The material dictates the process, the machinery and the frequency. These are the surfaces we work on and what each one calls for.

Marble, granite and natural stone

Porous and sensitive to acids. They take mechanical polishing to restore flatness, and crystallization to fix a lasting shine through a chemical reaction on the stone itself rather than an added layer. It is the most demanding treatment in terms of machinery, and the one that makes the most visible difference in lobbies and reception areas.

Porcelain tile

Harder and far less porous than natural stone, so it rarely needs polishing. What it does need is sealing, above all on the grout lines, which is where dirt and staining get in. Porcelain that looks dirty almost always has saturated grout, not damaged tile.

Terrazzo

Combines cement or resin with stone chips, so it responds well to polishing and crystallization — but the abrasive has to be graded to the aggregate. It is common in long-serving school and hospital facilities, where the goal is usually to recover a surface that was never treated in the first place.

Vinyl

Vinyl is not polished: it is maintained with waxing and buffing. The wax layer takes the wear and protects the sheet beneath. Once old layers build up the floor yellows, and the right move then is to strip and reapply, not to add another coat on top.

Concrete

Takes mechanical polishing and sealing. It is the usual surface in warehouses, plants and logistics areas, where the priority is not shine but closing the pore so it withstands heavy traffic and is easier to clean day to day.

What is the difference between polishing, crystallization, sealing and waxing?

The four terms get used interchangeably in a lot of quotes, and they are not the same thing:

  • Polishing. Progressive mechanical abrasion to remove scratches and restore flatness. It acts on the material itself.
  • Crystallization. A chemical reaction that hardens the stone's surface layer and produces shine. It adds no film: it transforms the surface.
  • Sealing. Closes the pore so the material stops absorbing liquids and stains. It is protection, not shine.
  • Waxing. Applies a sacrificial layer over the floor. The wear is taken by the wax, which is replaced periodically.

If a proposal does not state which of the four it will apply, and on which surface, there is no way to compare it with another. It is the same standard set out in the guide on how to choose a cleaning company.

How often does a floor need maintenance?

There is no single frequency: it depends on variables that have to be measured on site.

  • The material and the treatment applied
  • Traffic volume, and whether it is foot or equipment traffic
  • Exposure to sand, water or chemicals
  • Whether entrance matting is in place to trap particles
  • The finish the facility needs to maintain

That is why maintenance is contracted at an agreed frequency after assessing the surface, rather than bought as a one-off. A corporate tower lobby and a warehouse are not scheduled the same way even when the material is identical.

Is the floor slippery after treatment?

This is the most common objection, and a fair one: a badly executed shine can compromise safety. The work is delivered with a non-slip finish, which is a requirement of the process rather than an add-on. In critical areas it is worth having the proposal state in writing what finish is delivered and how the zone is signposted while the floor is being treated.

What should a floor care proposal include?

Beyond the price, a comparable proposal should specify:

  • Which material is being treated, and over what area
  • Which of the four processes applies in each zone
  • The machinery and consumables to be used
  • The non-slip finish being delivered
  • The working hours and how the area is signposted
  • The frequency of follow-up maintenance
  • Who supplies the consumables
